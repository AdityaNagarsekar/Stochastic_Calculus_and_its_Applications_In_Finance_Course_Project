\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We present a unified study of reinforcement learning for dynamic delta hedging,
integrating deep learning-based volatility forecasting and classical stochastic
calculus theory.  Three components are developed and connected end-to-end.

\textbf{Component 1} trains a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent to
hedge a European call option under geometric Brownian motion (GBM), benchmarked
against the analytically optimal Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) delta.  The
standard agent achieves a BSM-delta correlation of $0.946$ and mean absolute
hedging error (MAE) of $0.830$; six theory-motivated extensions are evaluated,
with domain randomisation ($\sigma\sim U(0.10,0.40)$) achieving the best RL
MAE of $\mathbf{0.723}$ and highest BSM correlation of $\mathbf{0.964}$.

\textbf{Component 2} forecasts 21-day forward realised volatility on Nifty~50
using a two-layer stacked LSTM, achieving out-of-sample MAE $0.0114$ and RMSE
$0.0203$, outperforming GARCH(1,1) by $41\%$ on MAE and $20\%$ on RMSE.  A
naive rv21 persistence baseline (MAE $0.0043$) outperforms all models at the
21-day horizon due to volatility clustering, and serves as the correct
explanation for the pricing horse-race results.

\textbf{Integration} connects both components: an LSTM-informed RL agent
trained in a varying-$\sigma$ environment achieves $\mathbf{MAE = 0.407}$
versus $1.435$ for a constant-$\sigma$ baseline when evaluated in their shared
natural habitat (72\% reduction, $p < 0.001$ by paired bootstrap).  On 51
non-overlapping windows of real Nifty paths spanning 2020--2024, BSM daily
hedging with rv21 (MAE 105.1) outperforms RL (MAE 137.5, $p=0.013$); replacing
rv21 by the LSTM forecast improves the analytic hedge to MAE 96.1, and a mixed
partial-rebalancing rule around the BSM-LSTM target achieves the best real-path
MAE of 88.3.  All strategies deteriorate sharply above transaction cost
$\kappa = 0.003$.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Motivation and Context}

The Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) model~\citep{Black1973,Merton1973} gives a
closed-form price for European options and an exact hedging rule under
idealised assumptions: frictionless continuous trading and constant volatility.
Two of those assumptions fail simultaneously in practice:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Transaction costs and discrete rebalancing.}  Continuous trading
        is costless in theory; daily rebalancing with proportional costs
        $\kappa|\Delta\delta|\cdot S$ creates a genuine optimisation problem
        whose solution has no known closed form.  The RL agent's task is to
        discover this solution.

  \item \textbf{Stochastic volatility.}  Equity volatility is random and
        mean-reverting.  Under a stochastic-vol model such as
        Heston~\citep{Heston1993}, BSM delta computed at a fixed $\sigma$ is
        provably suboptimal.  An agent that observes the current variance state
        can, in principle, do better.

  \item \textbf{Unknown volatility.}  Even if BSM were correct, $\sigma$ is
        unobservable and must be forecast.  Forecast accuracy translates
        directly to pricing and hedging quality via the Vega Greek.
\end{enumerate}

This project addresses all three layers within a single computational framework,
with stochastic calculus as the mathematical spine throughout.

\subsection{Structure and Contributions}

The project is organised into five parts, all sharing the BSM pricing engine
and Greeks ($\Delta$, $\Gamma$, $\mathcal{V}$):

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2cm,style=nextline]
  \item[Part I]  PPO agent for delta hedging under GBM and Heston, with BSM
        and bootstrap baselines.
  \item[Part II]  Six novel RL experiments varying reward function (MSE
        vs.\ It\^{o}-decomposed), environment (GBM vs.\ Heston), observation
        (with/without gamma), and training distribution (fixed vs.\
        domain-randomised $\sigma$).
  \item[Part III]  Stacked LSTM for Nifty~50 forward volatility forecasting,
        benchmarked against GARCH(1,1), a naive rv21 persistence baseline, and
        India~VIX implied volatility.
  \item[Part IV]  LSTM--RL integration with a natural-habitat evaluation that
        corrects a distributional alignment issue present in naive comparison.
  \item[Part V]  Extended real-path backtesting: 51 non-overlapping windows,
        including analytic, RL, and mixed BSM--LSTM execution rules, plus
        transaction-cost sensitivity and moneyness stress tests.
\end{description}

\section{Mathematical Foundations}
\label{sec:math}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Geometric Brownian Motion}

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \{\mathcal{F}_t\}, \mathbb{P})$ be a filtered
probability space satisfying the usual conditions.  A standard Brownian motion
$W = \{W_t,\,t\ge 0\}$ has independent, stationary Gaussian increments
$W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0,t-s)$ and continuous paths.

Under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$, the stock price satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  dS_t = r\,S_t\,dt + \sigma\,S_t\,dW_t,
  \label{eq:gbm}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is the continuously-compounded risk-free rate and $\sigma>0$ is
constant volatility.  Applying It\^{o}'s formula to $\ln S_t$:
\begin{equation}
  d\ln S_t = \Bigl(r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\Bigr)dt + \sigma\,dW_t,
  \label{eq:logS}
\end{equation}
giving the exact solution $S_t = S_0\exp[(r-\tfrac12\sigma^2)t + \sigma W_t]$.
Over one discrete step $\Delta t$ this becomes:
\begin{equation}
  S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t\exp\!\Bigl[\bigl(r-\tfrac12\sigma^2\bigr)\Delta t
                  + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\Bigr],
  \quad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).
  \label{eq:log_euler}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:log_euler} is the step used in the simulation environment ---
it is exact under GBM, not an approximation.

\subsection{Martingales and the Risk-Neutral Measure}
\label{sec:martingale}

A stochastic process $\{M_t\}$ is a \textbf{martingale} under measure
$\mathbb{P}$ with respect to $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}$ if
$\mathbb{E}^\mathbb{P}[M_t\,|\,\mathcal{F}_s] = M_s$ for all $s \le t$ ---
the process has no predictable drift.  Under $\mathbb{P}$, $S_t$ drifts at
rate $\mu$ and is not a martingale.

\textbf{Girsanov's theorem} guarantees the existence of a unique
equivalent measure $\mathbb{Q}$ (the \emph{risk-neutral measure}) under which
the discounted stock price $e^{-rt}S_t$ \emph{is} a martingale.
The change of measure is given by the Radon--Nikod\'{y}m derivative:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\mathbb{Q}}{d\mathbb{P}}\bigg|_{\mathcal{F}_T}
  = \exp\!\Bigl(-\theta\,W_T - \tfrac12\theta^2 T\Bigr),
  \quad \theta = \frac{\mu-r}{\sigma},
  \label{eq:girsanov}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the \textbf{market price of risk}.  Under $\mathbb{Q}$,
$\tilde{W}_t = W_t + \theta t$ is a standard Brownian motion, so
equation~\eqref{eq:gbm} becomes $dS_t = r S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,d\tilde{W}_t$.

The martingale property of $e^{-rt}S_t$ immediately gives the
\textbf{fundamental pricing formula}:
\begin{equation}
  V_0 = e^{-rT}\,\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[(S_T - K)^+],
  \label{eq:risk_neutral_price}
\end{equation}
which is the discounted risk-neutral expectation of the terminal payoff.
Equation~\eqref{eq:risk_neutral_price} is the theoretical foundation for
option pricing throughout this project: the BSM formula~\eqref{eq:bsm} is
the closed-form evaluation of this expectation under GBM, and the Heston
formula~\eqref{eq:heston_price} evaluates it under stochastic volatility
via the Gil-Pelaez inversion.  No-arbitrage pricing is therefore equivalent
to finding the unique $\mathbb{Q}$ that makes discounted asset prices martingales.

\subsection{It\^{o}'s Lemma and the Black-Scholes PDE}

\begin{theorem}[It\^{o}'s Lemma]
Let $f(t,x)\in C^{1,2}$.  If $X_t$ satisfies $dX_t = \mu\,dt + \nu\,dW_t$,
then:
\begin{equation}
  df(t,X_t) = \Bigl[\partial_t f + \mu\,\partial_x f
              + \tfrac12\nu^2\partial_{xx}f\Bigr]dt
              + \nu\,\partial_x f\,dW_t.
  \label{eq:ito}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Applying~\eqref{eq:ito} to an option value $V(t,S_t)$:
\begin{equation}
  dV = \Bigl[\partial_t V + rS\,\partial_S V
       + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2\,\partial_{SS}V\Bigr]dt
       + \sigma S\,\partial_S V\,dW_t.
  \label{eq:dV}
\end{equation}
A delta-hedged portfolio $\Pi = V - \delta S$ satisfies
$d\Pi = dV - \delta\,dS$.  Setting $\delta = \partial_S V$ eliminates the
$dW_t$ term.  No-arbitrage requires $d\Pi = r\Pi\,dt$, yielding the
\textbf{Black-Scholes PDE}:
\begin{equation}
  \partial_t V + rS\,\partial_S V + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2\,\partial_{SS}V
  - rV = 0.
  \label{eq:bspde}
\end{equation}

\subsection{BSM Formula and Greeks}

For a European call with strike $K$ and time-to-expiry $\tau = T-t$:
\begin{align}
  C(S,\tau) &= S\,\Phi(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_2),
  \label{eq:bsm}\\
  d_{1} &= \frac{\ln(S/K)+(r+\tfrac12\sigma^2)\tau}{\sigma\sqrt{\tau}},
  \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}.
  \label{eq:d1d2}
\end{align}
The Greeks used throughout this project:
\begin{alignat}{2}
  \Delta^* &= \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} = \Phi(d_1) \in (0,1),
  \label{eq:delta}\\
  \Gamma    &= \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}
             = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{S\sigma\sqrt{\tau}},
  \label{eq:gamma}\\
  \mathcal{V} &= \frac{\partial C}{\partial\sigma}
               = S\,\phi(d_1)\sqrt{\tau},
  \label{eq:vega}\\
  \Theta    &= \frac{\partial C}{\partial t}
             = -\frac{S\,\phi(d_1)\sigma}{2\sqrt{\tau}}
               - rKe^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_2),
  \label{eq:theta}
\end{alignat}
where $\phi = \Phi'$ is the standard normal PDF.
The BSM relation $\Theta + \tfrac12\sigma^2S^2\Gamma + rS\Delta - rC = 0$
connects all Greeks.

\textbf{Implied volatility} $\sigma_{\mathrm{impl}}$ is the unique positive
root of $C(\sigma) = C_{\mathrm{mkt}}$, solved via Brent's method.

\subsection{Hedging Error Decomposition}

Over step $\Delta t$ with hedge ratio $\delta$ (possibly $\neq\Delta^*$):
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon_t = \delta\,\Delta S - \Delta V
  = \underbrace{(\delta-\Delta^*)\Delta S}_{\text{controllable}}
    -\underbrace{\tfrac12\Gamma\bigl[(\Delta S)^2 - \sigma^2 S^2\Delta t\bigr]}
                _{\text{irreducible gamma noise}}.
  \label{eq:hedge_decomp}
\end{equation}
The gamma term has $\mathbb{E}[(\Delta S)^2] = \sigma^2 S^2\Delta t$ under
GBM (It\^{o}'s isometry), so its expectation is zero.
\emph{No choice of $\delta$ eliminates it.}
Its variance is $\tfrac12\sigma^4 S^4\Gamma^2(\Delta t)^2$, which peaks at-the-money
near expiry where $\Gamma$ is large.  Decomposition~\eqref{eq:hedge_decomp}
motivates both the It\^{o} reward (Section~\ref{sec:ito_reward}) and the gamma
observation (Section~\ref{sec:gamma_obs}).

\subsection{CRR Binomial Model}

The Cox--Ross--Rubinstein (CRR) binomial tree~\citep{CRR1979} discretises
stock price over $N$ steps of length $\Delta t = T/N$:
\begin{equation}
  u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}},
  \quad d = u^{-1},
  \quad p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t}-d}{u-d}.
  \label{eq:crr}
\end{equation}
Terminal payoffs are computed at all $N+1$ nodes; backward induction gives:
\begin{equation}
  V_{n,j} = e^{-r\Delta t}\bigl[p\,V_{n+1,j} + (1-p)\,V_{n+1,j+1}\bigr].
  \label{eq:crr_backward}
\end{equation}
For American options, exercise is checked at each node:
$V_{n,j}^{\mathrm{Am}} = \max(S_{n,j}-K,\; e^{-r\Delta t}[\cdots])$.
As $N\to\infty$, the CRR price converges to the BSM price.  We use $N=100$.

\subsection{Heston Stochastic Volatility Model}

Under the Heston model~\citep{Heston1993}, variance $V_t$ is a
mean-reverting CIR process correlated with the stock:
\begin{align}
  dS_t &= rS_t\,dt + \sqrt{V_t}\,S_t\,dW_1,
  \label{eq:heston_S}\\
  dV_t &= \kappa(\theta-V_t)\,dt + \xi\sqrt{V_t}\,dW_2,
  \label{eq:heston_V}\\
  dW_1\,dW_2 &= \rho\,dt.
  \label{eq:heston_corr}
\end{align}
Parameters used throughout: $\kappa=2.0$ (mean-reversion speed),
$\theta=0.04\approx(20\%)^2$ (long-run variance), $\xi=0.30$ (vol-of-vol),
$\rho=-0.70$ (leverage effect), $V_0=\theta$.
The Feller condition $2\kappa\theta > \xi^2$ ($0.16 > 0.09$) holds, ensuring
$V_t > 0$ almost surely.

\textbf{Simulation.}  Correlated Brownian increments are generated via
$W_1 = Z_1$, $W_2 = \rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,Z_2$ with
$Z_1,Z_2\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)$.
Variance uses the full-truncation Euler scheme~\citep{Andersen2008}:
\begin{equation}
  V_{t+\Delta t} = \max\!\Bigl(
    V_t + \kappa(\theta-V_t)\Delta t + \xi\sqrt{V_t}\sqrt{\Delta t}\,W_2,
    \;10^{-6}\Bigr).
  \label{eq:heston_euler}
\end{equation}

The first term $\kappa(\theta-V_t)\Delta t$ is the \textbf{mean-reversion drift}:
when $V_t<\theta$ it pushes variance upward; when $V_t>\theta$ it pulls it down.
The second term $\xi\sqrt{V_t}\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_2$ is the \textbf{stochastic
shock}, whose magnitude scales with $\sqrt{V_t}$ so vol-of-vol is amplified in
high-variance regimes.  The $\max(\cdot,10^{-6})$ floor is a numerical safeguard:
a large negative draw can make the Euler update negative, invalidating
$\sqrt{V_t}$, so truncation is applied rather than reflection.

\textbf{Semi-analytic pricing.}
The Heston characteristic function is:
\begin{align}
  \Psi_j(\phi) &= \exp\!\Bigl[C_j(\tau,\phi) + D_j(\tau,\phi)V_0
                 + i\phi\ln S\Bigr],
  \label{eq:heston_cf}\\
  C_j &= ri\phi\tau + \frac{\kappa\theta}{\xi^2}
         \!\left[(b_j - \rho\xi i\phi + d_j)\tau
         - 2\ln\!\frac{1-g_j e^{d_j\tau}}{1-g_j}\right],
  \label{eq:heston_C}\\
  D_j &= \frac{b_j-\rho\xi i\phi+d_j}{\xi^2}\cdot
         \frac{1-e^{d_j\tau}}{1-g_j e^{d_j\tau}},
  \label{eq:heston_D}\\
  d_j &= \sqrt{(\rho\xi i\phi - b_j)^2 - \xi^2(2u_j i\phi - \phi^2)},
  \quad g_j = \frac{b_j-\rho\xi i\phi+d_j}{b_j-\rho\xi i\phi-d_j},
\end{align}
with $u_1=\tfrac12,\,b_1=\kappa-\rho\xi$ and $u_2=-\tfrac12,\,b_2=\kappa$.
The call price follows from Gil-Pelaez inversion:
\begin{equation}
  C_{\mathrm{Heston}} = SP_1 - Ke^{-r\tau}P_2,
  \quad P_j = \frac12 + \frac{1}{\pi}
              \int_0^\infty \mathrm{Re}\!\left[
              \frac{e^{-i\phi\ln K}\,\Psi_j(\phi)}{i\phi}\right]d\phi.
  \label{eq:heston_price}
\end{equation}
Numerical integration uses \texttt{scipy.integrate.quad} on $[10^{-6},200]$
with 200 subdivisions and absolute tolerance $10^{-4}$.

\textbf{Why the characteristic function?}  Under Heston, $S_T$ is not
log-normal, so $\mathbb{E}[(S_T-K)^+]$ has no elementary closed form.
However, the characteristic function $\Psi(\phi)=\mathbb{E}[e^{i\phi\ln S_T}]$
\emph{is} available in closed form for affine models --- the pricing PDE
becomes an ODE in Fourier space with solution $(C_j, D_j)$.  Gil-Pelaez
inversion then recovers the two risk-neutral probabilities $P_1, P_2$
via numerical quadrature, giving the same structural decomposition as BSM
($C = SP_1 - Ke^{-r\tau}P_2$) but with Fourier integrals replacing $\Phi(d_1), \Phi(d_2)$.

\section{Part I: RL Delta Hedging Under GBM}
\label{sec:p1}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Environment Design}

The \texttt{HedgingEnv} Gymnasium environment simulates delta hedging over
$n=21$ discrete steps ($T = 21/252$ years, one trading month).

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2cm]
  \item[State] $\mathbf{s}_t = [S_t/K,\;\tau_t/T,\;\delta_{t-1}]$
    (moneyness, normalised time-to-expiry, previous hedge ratio).
    These three numbers are sufficient to compute BSM delta analytically.
  \item[Action] $a_t = \delta_t \in [0,1]$, the new hedge ratio.
  \item[Dynamics] Exact log-Euler GBM step~\eqref{eq:log_euler}.
  \item[Reward] $r_t = -\varepsilon_t^2 - \kappa|\delta_t-\delta_{t-1}|S_t$,
    with $\kappa = 0.001$.  The squared error penalises large mistakes
    disproportionately (risk-sensitive) and corresponds to the
    \emph{variance-optimal hedging} objective from stochastic control theory.
    The second term imposes a proportional transaction cost equal to the
    dollar volume traded; without it, the agent would rebalance at every step
    with no regard for cost.
  \item[Initialisation] $S_0\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0.9K,\,1.1K)$;
    portfolio funded at BSM price $C(S_0,T,r,\sigma)$.
\end{description}

\textbf{Parameters}: $K = S_0 = 100$, $T = 21/252$, $r = 5\%$,
$\sigma = 20\%$, $\kappa = 0.001$, $n_{\mathrm{steps}} = 21$.

\textbf{Terminal P\&L}: at expiry, the terminal hedging error is:
\begin{equation}
  \Pi_T^{\mathrm{hedge}} = \Pi_T - \max(S_T - K, 0),
  \label{eq:terminal_pnl}
\end{equation}
where $\Pi_T$ is the running portfolio value.  A perfect hedge achieves
$\Pi_T^{\mathrm{hedge}} = 0$.

\subsection{PPO Agent}

Proximal Policy Optimization~\citep{Schulman2017} via Stable-Baselines3
\citep{Raffin2021} with \texttt{MlpPolicy} (architecture: [128, 64], Tanh
activation).  The clipped surrogate objective is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)
  = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\min\!\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t,\;
    \mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta),1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)\hat{A}_t\right)\right],
  \label{eq:ppo}
\end{equation}
where $r_t(\theta)=\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)/\pi_{\theta_\mathrm{old}}(a_t|s_t)$
is the probability ratio, $\varepsilon=0.2$ is the clip parameter, and
$\hat{A}_t$ is the Generalised Advantage Estimate~\citep{Schulman2016}
($\lambda_{\mathrm{GAE}}=0.95$).  Training: $\gamma=0.99$, lr$=10^{-4}$
(Adam), 300,000 environment steps.

The clipping mechanism prevents destructive policy updates: when
$r_t > 1+\varepsilon$ (large probability increase), the objective is capped so
further increase yields no additional gradient, and symmetrically for
$r_t < 1-\varepsilon$.  In a noisy financial reward (driven by $(\Delta S)^2$
and gamma noise), unconstrained policy gradient can oscillate wildly;
PPO's conservative updates are the primary reason the agent converges to a
stable hedging policy.

\subsection{BSM Baselines}

\texttt{BSM daily}: at each step set $\delta_t = \Delta^* = \Phi(d_1)$
evaluated at $(S_t, \tau_t, \sigma)$.  Theoretically optimal under GBM with no
transaction costs.  \texttt{BSM weekly}: same formula, rebalances every 5 steps
(4 trades), trading hedging accuracy for lower cost.

\textbf{LSTM-guided BSM delta}: uses per-step LSTM vol forecasts $\hat\sigma_t$
as the sigma input to $\Delta^*$, evaluated on 519 LSTM-forecast windows drawn
from the Nifty test period (Section~\ref{sec:p3}).

\textbf{Bootstrap baseline}: episodes use 21 consecutive real Nifty
log-returns (block bootstrap from test period) as the price path; BSM delta is
applied at the rolling-21d realised vol.  Unlike random IID sampling, the block
bootstrap preserves the autocorrelation and vol-clustering structure of actual
market returns.  Its higher MAE relative to GBM-BSM directly measures the
cost of model misspecification: the gap arises from fat tails, vol clustering,
and non-normality that GBM-trained strategies have never encountered.

\section{Part II: Novel RL Experiments}
\label{sec:novel}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Part~I established that RL cannot beat the analytic BSM hedge under GBM, but
left open the question of \emph{why}: is it the reward signal, the environment,
the observation space, or the training distribution?  Part~II answers this
through six ablations that vary each design choice in turn.  All experiments use
the same PPO hyperparameters as Part~I unless stated.

\subsection{Experiment A: GBM + MSE Reward (Baseline)}

The baseline novel experiment reproduces the Part~I RL agent within the novel
experiment framework for consistent comparison.  MAE $= 0.830$, BSM-delta
correlation $= 0.947$.

\subsection{Experiment B: It\^{o}-Decomposed Reward}
\label{sec:ito_reward}

\textbf{Motivation.}  From decomposition~\eqref{eq:hedge_decomp}, the gamma
P\&L
\begin{equation}
  G_t = \tfrac12\Gamma_t\bigl[(\Delta S)^2 - \sigma^2 S_t^2\Delta t\bigr]
  \label{eq:gamma_pnl}
\end{equation}
has $\mathbb{E}[G_t] = 0$ but $\mathrm{Var}(G_t) = \tfrac12\sigma^4 S^4\Gamma^2(\Delta t)^2 >0$.
It adds variance to the per-step reward that the agent cannot reduce.
The It\^{o} reward subtracts $G_t$ before squaring:
\begin{equation}
  r_t^{\mathrm{It\hat{o}}} = -(\varepsilon_t - G_t)^2 - \kappa|\delta_t-\delta_{t-1}|S_t.
  \label{eq:ito_reward}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Result.}  MAE $= 0.926$ --- \emph{worse} than A ($p=0.004$, paired
bootstrap).  At convergence $\delta\to\Delta^*$, the hedge error $\varepsilon_t$
\emph{is} the gamma P\&L $G_t$; subtracting it drives the controllable reward
signal to zero, removing the gradient.  Additionally, $\Gamma_t\to\infty$ as
$\tau\to 0$ (ATM near-expiry), causing numerical noise in $G_t$ that
overwhelms the variance-reduction benefit.
In short, the It\^{o} reward \emph{removes noise but also removes signal}: the
original $-\varepsilon_t^2$ reward, though noisy, retains a nonzero gradient
even near optimum.  The It\^{o} reward becomes essentially flat when
$\delta\approx\Delta^*$, starving the policy of learning signal precisely when
it is close to the correct solution.

\subsection{Experiment C: Heston + MSE Reward}

\textbf{Motivation.}  Under GBM, BSM delta is provably optimal so RL cannot
improve over the analytic benchmark.  Under Heston, the vol state $\sqrt{V_t}$
is observable but BSM delta ignores it --- creating genuine scope for RL to
outperform.

\textbf{Observation extension.}  The 4D observation
$\mathbf{s}_t = [S/K,\;\tau/T,\;\delta_{\mathrm{prev}},\;\sqrt{V_t}/\sqrt{\theta}]$
provides the current vol regime relative to the long-run mean: values $>1$
signal elevated vol, $<1$ subdued vol.  Heston variance is simulated via
equation~\eqref{eq:heston_euler}.

\textbf{Result.}  MAE $= 0.790$, corr $= 0.963$.  The improved correlation
(vs.\ A: 0.947) reflects that the richer environment provides more
structure for the agent to learn from.  The MAE improvement ($-4.8\%$)
represents a genuine advantage of the stochastic-vol setting.

\subsection{Experiment D: Heston + It\^{o} Reward}

\textbf{Result.}  MAE $= 0.790$, corr $= 0.958$.  Under Heston, the
It\^{o} reward makes no significant difference ($p = 0.984$).  The richer
environment dynamics provide sufficient training signal that the reward
decomposition neither helps nor hurts.

\subsection{Experiment E: GBM + It\^{o} Reward + Gamma Observation (500k steps)}
\label{sec:gamma_obs}

\textbf{Motivation.}  Dollar-gamma $\Gamma_t S_t = \phi(d_1)/(\sigma\sqrt{\tau_t})$
is large ATM near-expiry and small deep ITM/OTM.  An agent that observes it
can rebalance conservatively in low-gamma regimes and precisely when
$\Gamma$ is high.

\textbf{Implementation.}  The gamma observation is the normalised
dollar-gamma:
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_t^{\mathrm{obs}} = \frac{\phi(d_1)}{\phi(0)}
  = \Gamma_t \cdot S_t \cdot \sigma\sqrt{\tau_t} / 0.3989 \in [0,1],
  \label{eq:gamma_obs}
\end{equation}
which equals 1 exactly at-the-money at any $\tau$ and $\sigma$, providing a
normalisation-invariant regime signal.  The observation is 4D:
$\mathbf{s}_t = [S/K,\;\tau/T,\;\delta_{\mathrm{prev}},\;\gamma_t^{\mathrm{obs}}]$.
Training is extended to 500k steps (vs.\ 300k for A--D) to ensure convergence.

\textbf{Result.}  MAE $= 0.764$ (vs.\ B at 300k steps: 0.926 --- improvement
of 17.5\%, $p<0.001$).  Extended training alone accounts for part of this
improvement; the gamma observation provides structural signal that accelerates
learning in high-$\Gamma$ regimes.
The contrast with Experiment B is instructive: the It\^{o} reward alone hurts
because it weakens the gradient without replacing it with structure.  Adding
$\gamma^{\mathrm{obs}}$ solves this: the agent now knows \emph{where} errors
are most costly (high-$\Gamma$ ATM near expiry) and can focus its accuracy
budget on those states, making the weaker but cleaner reward signal sufficient.

\subsection{Experiment F: Domain Randomisation ($\sigma\sim U(0.10,0.40)$)}

\textbf{Motivation.}  All previous experiments fix $\sigma=0.20$ during
training and evaluation.  An agent trained at a single vol may memorise
that regime rather than learning a genuine delta function.  Domain
randomisation samples $\sigma$ uniformly at each episode reset, forcing the
agent to learn a vol-conditioned policy.

\textbf{Sigma observation.}  To enable conditioning, a sigma proxy
$\sigma_t^{\mathrm{obs}} = \sigma_t / 0.20$ is appended (normalised to
the reference vol).  The observation is 4D:
$\mathbf{s}_t = [S/K,\;\tau/T,\;\delta_{\mathrm{prev}},\;\sigma_t^{\mathrm{obs}}]$.

\textbf{Result.}  MAE $= 0.723$, BSM-delta correlation $= 0.964$ ---
\textbf{best among all RL variants}.  The higher correlation confirms the
policy is more BSM-aligned when trained across the full vol distribution
rather than a single point.

\section{Part III: LSTM Volatility Forecasting}
\label{sec:p3}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Motivation: Why Volatility Forecasting Matters}

Parts~I and~II showed that RL's difficulty partly stems from using a fixed,
misspecified $\sigma$.  Part~III addresses this directly: if we can supply better
volatility inputs to the analytic hedge, how much does performance improve?

BSM price is monotone in $\sigma$.  A vol forecast error $\Delta\sigma$
translates to a pricing error of magnitude $\mathcal{V}\cdot|\Delta\sigma|$.
For a 1-month ATM call with $S=K=100$, $\sigma=0.20$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{V} = 100\cdot\phi(0)\cdot\sqrt{21/252} \approx 11.5.
\end{equation}
A 1 percentage-point vol error produces a \$0.115 pricing error ---
substantial relative to option premiums of \$1--\$3.  The Vega surface
(Figure~\ref{fig:vega}) shows this sensitivity across maturities.

\subsection{Data and Features}

\textbf{Dataset.} Nifty~50 daily closing prices (\^{}NSEI, Yahoo Finance),
2010--2024, approximately 3,700 trading days.  Temporal split:
70\% train / 15\% validation / 15\% test (strictly chronological).

\textbf{Features.}
\begin{align}
  r_t &= \ln(S_t/S_{t-1}), \quad |r_t|,\label{eq:feat_ret}\\
  \mathrm{RV}_5(t)   &= \sqrt{252}\cdot\mathrm{std}(r_{t-4},\ldots,r_t),\\
  \mathrm{RV}_{21}(t) &= \sqrt{252}\cdot\mathrm{std}(r_{t-20},\ldots,r_t),\\
  \mathrm{rv\_ratio}(t) &= \mathrm{RV}_5(t)/\mathrm{RV}_{21}(t)
    \;\text{(clipped to [0.1, 10])},\\
  \mathrm{mom}_5(t) &= \sum_{i=1}^5 r_{t-i+1}
    \;\text{(clipped to $\pm0.30$)}.
\end{align}
The \texttt{rv\_ratio} captures the \textbf{volatility term structure}: values
$>1$ signal a spike (short-horizon vol exceeds long-horizon trend), values $<1$
a cooling regime.  This is the single most informative feature because
it encodes \emph{regime change} rather than just level, which is precisely what
persistence-based models like GARCH miss.  GARCH maintains a single variance
state; the LSTM sees the relative dynamics across two horizons and can
distinguish ``vol is high but falling'' from ``vol is high and rising.''
Features are StandardScaled using train-set statistics only.

\textbf{Target.} Forward 21-day realised volatility:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma^*_t = \sqrt{252}\cdot\mathrm{std}(r_{t+1},\ldots,r_{t+21}).
  \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
Sequences of length 60 trading days are used as LSTM input.

\subsection{Model Architecture}

Two-layer stacked LSTM~\citep{Hochreiter1997}:
\begin{align}
  \mathbf{h}^{(1)}_t &= \mathrm{LSTM}_1(\mathbf{x}_t,\,\mathbf{h}^{(1)}_{t-1}),
    \quad \mathbf{h}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{64},\\
  \mathbf{h}^{(2)}_t &= \mathrm{LSTM}_2(\mathbf{h}^{(1)}_t,\,\mathbf{h}^{(2)}_{t-1}),
    \quad \mathbf{h}^{(2)}\in\mathbb{R}^{32},\\
  \hat{\sigma}^*     &= \mathrm{Softplus}(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{h}^{(2)}_{60}+b).
\end{align}
Dropout $p=0.20$ between layers.  Softplus $= \log(1+e^x)$ ensures
$\hat\sigma>0$; output bias initialised to 0.15 (approximate mean Nifty vol)
so predictions start in the correct range from epoch 1.

\textbf{Training.}  Adam~\citep{Kingma2015}, lr $= 10^{-3}$,
ReduceLROnPlateau (factor 0.5, patience 7), early stopping (patience 15),
gradient clipping (max norm 1.0), batch size 64, MSE loss.
Best validation-loss checkpoint is restored for evaluation.

\subsection{Baselines}

\textbf{GARCH(1,1).}  Conditional variance:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha_1\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta_1\sigma_{t-1}^2.
  \label{eq:garch}
\end{equation}
Industry-standard benchmark~\citep{Bollerslev1986,Engle1982}.  Fitted via
maximum likelihood on an expanding window with no look-ahead.

\textbf{Naive rv21 persistence.}  Uses $\mathrm{RV}_{21}(t)$ (trailing
21-day realised vol) as the forecast for the next 21 days' realised vol.
This captures the key economic insight: the trailing window $[t-20, t]$
overlaps 86\% with the forward target window $[t+1, t+21]$, so under
strong volatility clustering the persistence baseline has an inherent
informational advantage.

\textbf{India VIX.}  Daily closing levels of India VIX (\^{}INDIAVIX,
Yahoo Finance), divided by 100 for decimal format.  Represents the market's
risk-neutral implied vol and includes a variance risk premium.

\textbf{Discussion.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{LSTM beats GARCH} (MAE $-41\%$, RMSE $-20\%$, directional
        accuracy $+2.4$pp).  The multi-scale \texttt{rv\_ratio} feature is
        likely the primary driver: GARCH has a single variance state, while
        the LSTM integrates mean-reversion signals across two horizons.

  \item \textbf{rv21 outperforms LSTM} (MAE 0.0043 vs.\ 0.0114).  This is
        expected and not a modelling failure.  The trailing 21-day window
        $[t-20, t]$ overlaps with 86\% of the forward target window $[t+1, t+21]$,
        giving it an inherent informational advantage: under Nifty's strong
        volatility clustering (Hurst exponent $\approx 0.65$), the past 21 days
        of realised vol is already a near-optimal proxy for the next 21 days.
        Any more complex model must improve on something that is already very
        close to optimal, which is a fundamentally hard task.  The rv21 baseline
        is included as a transparency control, not as a benchmark the LSTM must
        beat; the LSTM's value lies in longer-horizon regime forecasting and
        in informing the RL agent (Section~\ref{sec:integration}).

  \item \textbf{India VIX worst} (MAE 0.0344).  Implied vol is computed under
        the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$, not the physical measure
        $\mathbb{P}$.  By Girsanov's theorem, the market price of risk
        $\theta = (\mu-r)/\sigma$ drives a wedge between the two measures:
        investors pay a \emph{variance risk premium} to hedge volatility
        exposure, causing implied vol to systematically exceed realised vol by
        $\approx 4$--$6\%$.  India VIX is therefore a biased predictor of
        future realised volatility, which explains its poor MAE relative to
        physically-estimated models.

  \item \textbf{Directional accuracy near 50\%}.  All models are near
        coin-flip accuracy for predicting the sign of the next vol change.
        This is expected and reflects a fundamental property of vol dynamics:
        \emph{volatility is predictable in level but not in direction.}
        Persistence (volatility clustering) makes the level sticky ---
        tomorrow's vol is likely close to today's --- but mean reversion
        simultaneously pushes elevated vol downward and subdued vol upward.
        These two forces roughly cancel, leaving
        $\mathbb{P}(\sigma_{t+1}>\sigma_t)\approx 0.5$.  Directional accuracy
        is therefore a poor metric for volatility models; what matters is
        level accuracy (RMSE, MAE), where LSTM clearly beats GARCH.
\end{itemize}

\section{Part IV: LSTM--RL Integration}
\label{sec:integration}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Design}

Part~III produced a per-day LSTM volatility forecast $\hat\sigma_t$ for each
Nifty window.  Part~IV asks: can an RL agent exploit this richer vol signal in
a way the analytic BSM formula cannot?  The LSTM vol schedule
$\{\hat\sigma_1,\ldots,\hat\sigma_{21}\}$ replaces the
fixed $\sigma$ in the hedging environment (\texttt{LSTMHedgingEnv}).  At each
step $t$, the environment uses $\hat\sigma_t$ to simulate the GBM step,
price the option $V_t$ for the reward, and supply $\sigma_t^{\mathrm{obs}} =
\hat\sigma_t/0.20$ as the fourth observation dimension.  The RL agent trains on
windows drawn from the LSTM validation predictions; evaluation uses the test
period.

\subsection{Distribution Shift Issue and Natural Habitat Evaluation}

A na\"{i}ve comparison evaluates both agents at a fixed $\sigma=0.20$.
This is unfair: the LSTM-RL agent was trained on varying-$\sigma$ inputs;
evaluating it at a constant $\sigma$ creates distribution shift that degrades
its performance.  Conversely, the constant-$\sigma$ agent sees its training
distribution and performs well.

The \textbf{natural habitat evaluation} tests both agents in
\texttt{LSTMHedgingEnv} with $\sigma$ drawn from LSTM test predictions ---
the actual deployment environment for the LSTM-RL agent.  This is the correct
comparison for assessing whether LSTM vol information is genuinely useful.

\section{Part V: Real-Path Backtesting}
\label{sec:backtest}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Methodology}

Parts~I--IV evaluated strategies in simulation.  Part~V brings all
components to real Nifty~50 data: does the advantage of the analytic hedge
over RL survive on real price paths, and does the LSTM-guided execution
rule improve further?

We evaluate hedging strategies on real Nifty~50 price paths to test
performance outside the GBM assumption.  Using the combined validation+test
period (2020--2024), we extract 51 non-overlapping 21-day windows
(stride $= n_{\mathrm{steps}} = 21$).  The starting price is aligned to
the nearest round Nifty-style strike.  Terminal P\&L is computed per
equation~\eqref{eq:terminal_pnl} with $\kappa=0.001$.

The original comparison uses the rolling 21-day realised volatility rv21 at
window start as the hedge sigma.  We also evaluate two extensions using the
same 51 windows: (i)~\textit{BSM-LSTM}, which replaces rv21 by the LSTM
forecast at the start of the window, and (ii)~\textit{partial-BSM-LSTM}, a
mixed execution rule around the BSM-LSTM target,
\[
  \Delta_{t+1} =
  \begin{cases}
    \Delta_t, & |\Delta_t^{\star}-\Delta_t| \le \theta, \\
    \Delta_t + \alpha(\Delta_t^{\star}-\Delta_t), & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
\]
where $\Delta_t^{\star}$ is the BSM delta computed with the LSTM sigma.
The hyperparameters $(\alpha,\theta)$ are selected on the validation windows
only, then reported on the combined val+test sample.

Statistical power: 51 non-overlapping windows covering five distinct volatility
regimes (COVID recovery, 2021 rally, 2022 correction, 2023 recovery,
2024 normalisation) provide sufficient variation for a meaningful paired test.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Summary of Main Findings}

Taken together, the five parts tell a coherent story: stochastic calculus
provides the structure that makes machine learning useful.  RL in isolation
fails to generalise; RL with analytic structure, learned vol, and distributional
alignment succeeds.  The seven findings below trace this arc.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Under GBM, BSM delta is near-optimal; RL approximates but does
        not surpass it.}  The best RL variant (domain randomisation, MAE 0.723)
        achieves a BSM-delta correlation of 0.964 but an MAE three times higher
        than BSM daily (0.284).  The LSTM-guided BSM (MAE 0.227) is the
        strongest hedging strategy in simulation.

  \item \textbf{The It\^{o} reward decomposition does not improve RL training.}
        Removing the gamma P\&L from the reward hurts under GBM ($p=0.004$)
        and is neutral under Heston ($p=0.984$).  The practical lesson: reward
        shaping based on theoretical decompositions requires empirical
        validation.

  \item \textbf{Domain randomisation is the most impactful RL design choice.}
        Training across $\sigma\in[0.10,0.40]$ produces a vol-conditioned
        policy with higher BSM-delta correlation and lower MAE than any fixed-$\sigma$
        variant, at no additional computational cost.

  \item \textbf{Distributional alignment is critical for fair evaluation.}
        LSTM-RL evaluated in its natural habitat (varying-$\sigma$ environment)
        reduces MAE by 72\% and CVaR$_{95}$ by 64\% relative to a
        constant-$\sigma$ baseline ($p<0.001$, both metrics).  Under a
        na\"{i}ve constant-$\sigma$ comparison the same agent appears marginally
        worse, illustrating how evaluation design can reverse apparent conclusions.

  \item \textbf{On real Nifty paths, analytic structure beats stand-alone RL.}
        Base BSM with rv21 (MAE 105.1) beats RL PPO (137.5, $p=0.013$), while
        BSM-LSTM improves further to 96.1.  The best real-path result is a
        mixed execution rule around the BSM-LSTM target (MAE 88.3), indicating
        that adaptivity is most useful for \emph{how much} to rebalance, not
        for replacing the analytic hedge outright.

  \item \textbf{Transaction costs are the binding constraint above
        $\kappa = 0.003$.}  Both methods degrade severely; at $\kappa=0.01$
        MAEs are $3\times$ higher than at the baseline $\kappa=0.001$.

  \item \textbf{LSTM beats GARCH significantly; rv21 dominates at the 21-day
        horizon.}  The 41\% MAE improvement over GARCH is genuine; rv21's
        advantage at the 21-day horizon is a vol-clustering effect (86\%
        window overlap), not a modelling failure.  In the pricing horse race,
        lagged implied vol and historical rv21 are nearly tied (MAE 18.7 vs
        18.6), both far ahead of LSTM (30.3) and GARCH (43.6).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{GBM-trained RL on real paths.}  The model--reality gap is
        substantial; all stand-alone PPO and DQN variants underperform the
        analytic real-path baselines.  Adding fat-tailed simulations
        (jump-diffusion, CGMY), walk-forward real-returns training, or a
        controller-style objective rather than direct delta prediction would
        likely improve real-path performance.

  \item \textbf{BSM approximation in Heston environment.}  The environment
        prices options using BSM at $\sqrt{V_t}$ rather than the full Heston
        formula~\eqref{eq:heston_price}.  This introduces reward bias;
        exact Heston pricing would require numerical integration at every step
        (computationally expensive during training).

  \item \textbf{Synthetic market prices in moneyness backtest.}  The pricing
        horse-race uses BSM proxy prices with a 4\% risk premium plus noise.
        Real NSE option chains would provide a more stringent test.

  \item \textbf{Statistical power of GBM vs.\ Heston comparison.}  Heston
        experiments are trained for 1M steps (vs.\ 300k for GBM), yet the
        MAE difference ($p=0.216$) remains non-significant.  The null result
        likely reflects that the Heston environment's additional complexity is
        offset by the richer training signal from the vol-state observation;
        a larger episode budget or variance-reduction techniques may be needed
        to detect a smaller true effect.
\end{enumerate}

%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This project demonstrates that the tools of stochastic calculus have direct
practical value in the design and evaluation of modern machine learning systems
for derivatives trading.

It\^{o}'s lemma provides the hedging error decomposition~\eqref{eq:hedge_decomp}
that motivates both the It\^{o} reward (Experiment B/D) and the gamma observation
(Experiment E).  The Heston model promotes $\sigma$ to a stochastic process,
creating an environment where BSM delta is provably suboptimal and RL can
exploit the observable variance state.  The BSM Greeks $\Delta$, $\Gamma$,
and $\mathcal{V}$ connect all three project components: $\Delta^*$ is the
analytic target that RL approximates; $\Gamma$ is the regime signal that guides
rebalancing; $\mathcal{V}$ quantifies how LSTM vol forecast errors propagate
into pricing errors.

The strongest numerical results are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item LSTM beats GARCH by 41\% on MAE and 20\% on RMSE --- a significant
        improvement over the industry-standard benchmark.
  \item LSTM-guided BSM delta achieves the lowest simulation hedging MAE
        (0.227), 20\% below BSM daily.
  \item On real Nifty paths, BSM-LSTM improves over base BSM (MAE 96.1 vs.\
        105.1), and the best overall real-path result is a mixed execution
        rule around the BSM-LSTM target (MAE 88.3).
  \item LSTM-RL reduces MAE by 72\% in the natural habitat evaluation
        ($p<0.001$), demonstrating the value of vol information when
        distributional alignment is respected.
  \item Domain randomisation over $\sigma\in[0.10,0.40]$ achieves the
        best stand-alone RL policy (MAE 0.723, corr $= 0.964$), confirming
        that training distribution breadth is more valuable than reward
        engineering.
\end{itemize}

The central lesson is one of alignment: the most important design decision in
a learned hedging system is not the reward function or network architecture,
but whether the training and deployment distributions match.  When they do,
learned policies can compress substantial advantages over constant-$\sigma$
baselines.
